## Summary

A ScienceDaily piece published August 1, 2026, summarizes new research out
of the University of Cambridge showing, mathematically, that no electoral
system can be perfectly fair. Researchers Sebastian Tim Holdum (University
of Copenhagen) and Frederik Ravn Klausen (University of Cambridge) proved
an "impossibility theorem" for two-tier electoral systems: once enough
political parties are competing, no voting method can simultaneously
guarantee that local election winners get local seats, that a party's
national seat total matches its share of the national vote, and that the
parliament stays a fixed size ("Mathematicians Prove"). The two
mathematicians were partly motivated by Denmark's 2022 general election,
where a regional seat was not corrected by compensatory seats for the
first time since 1947, effectively deciding the election by a single seat
despite Denmark's proportional system. As Klausen put it, "Essentially, it
almost broke the system... in a two-tier system, that isn't meant to
happen. It increased the drama that this single seat decided the
election" (qtd. in "Mathematicians Prove"). Germany faced a related
problem, where balancing regional representation and proportionality
caused the Bundestag to grow before reforms limited its size in 2023. The
researchers propose an alternative algorithm, "geographically ranked
guaranteed proportionality," in which a party's national seat total would
be set purely by its overall vote share, regardless of where those votes
were cast, as a way to soften — though not eliminate — the trade-offs.

## Annotations

- The proof covers "two-tier" systems (local seats + national/
  compensatory seats), which is how many proportional democracies,
  including Denmark and Germany, are structured.
- Three properties in tension: (1) local winners get local seats, (2)
  national seat share matches national vote share, (3) fixed parliament
  size. Theorem: you cannot guarantee all three once enough parties
  compete.
- Real 2022 Denmark example: a single uncorrected regional seat decided
  the national election outcome — first time this had happened since
  1947.
- Germany's Bundestag ballooned in size trying to satisfy both
  proportionality and regional fairness, before a 2023 size cap reform.
- Proposed fix ("geographically ranked guaranteed proportionality") ties
  national seats strictly to vote share — trades away some of the local-
  representation guarantee to gain proportionality and fixed size.

## Personal Response & Questions

This connects directly to what we've been doing in Algebra 2 with
systems and constraints — it's basically a proof that you can't optimize
for three variables at once when they pull against each other, the same
way you can't always satisfy every constraint in a system of inequalities.
It's a little unsettling that "perfectly fair" elections are
mathematically impossible, not just politically hard.

1. If Holdum and Klausen's theorem proves no system can guarantee all
   three fairness properties at once, how should a country even decide
   which one — fixed size, proportionality, or local representation — to
   give up?
2. Would the "geographically ranked guaranteed proportionality" algorithm
   they propose actually be simple enough for voters to understand and
   trust, or does making elections mathematically fairer risk making them
   more confusing to the people voting in them?
