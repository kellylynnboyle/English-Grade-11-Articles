## Summary

Deadline's review of *Coyote vs. Acme* covers the strange path this Looney
Tunes film took to reach theaters. Warner Bros. finished the live-action/
animation hybrid — starring Will Forte as an ACME-suing lawyer and John Cena
as a corporate rival — but shelved it in November 2023, writing off the
roughly $70–72 million production budget for a tax benefit instead of
releasing it ("Coyote vs. Acme Review"). After Will Forte publicly said the
film was likely never coming out, independent distributor Ketchup
Entertainment bought it for a reported $50 million and gave it a full
nationwide theatrical release on August 28, 2026. Critics have responded
well: the film built a 94% score on Rotten Tomatoes and a 75 on Metacritic,
with multiple reviewers calling it one of the strongest live-action/
animation hybrids since *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* ("Coyote vs. Acme
Review").

## Annotations

- Finished film, full ~$70–72M budget already spent — shelved anyway in
  Nov. 2023 purely for a tax write-off, not because it tested poorly.
- Ketchup Entertainment's $50M purchase reversed the write-off decision;
  this is the same distributor that saved *The Day the Earth Blew Up* from
  a similar fate.
- Blends traditional 2D-style Looney Tunes animation with live-action
  performers (Forte, Cena) — a format that's rare and expensive to pull
  off well.
- Critical reception (94% RT, 75 Metacritic) is unusually strong for a
  film the studio itself gave up on.
- Release timing: wide theatrical release the same day this annotation
  was drafted (Aug. 28, 2026).

## Personal Response & Questions

It's strange to think a completed, well-made film can just get "canceled"
for accounting reasons rather than creative ones — the movie didn't fail
with audiences, because audiences never got the chance to see it until a
different company stepped in. That makes me wonder how many other shelved
projects are sitting in a studio vault right now for the same reason.

1. If a studio can write off a fully finished, well-reviewed film for a tax
   break, what does that say about how "success" is measured in the
   animation/film industry — creative quality, or accounting?
2. Now that *Coyote vs. Acme* shows a shelved film can still find an
   audience when sold to another distributor, will more studios start
   treating unreleased films as assets to sell off rather than write off?
