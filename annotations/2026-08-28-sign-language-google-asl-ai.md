## Summary

In an August 20, 2026 Forbes article, Keely Cat-Wells reports on Google
DeepMind's new SL2T ("Sign Language to Text") model, which now powers
real-time American Sign Language translation on the Pixel 11 phone. The
feature lets Deaf and hard-of-hearing users sign directly into their
phone's camera through the Gboard keyboard or the Live Transcribe app
instead of typing, and it works inside any app that accepts text input,
from web searches to emails to conversations with Google's Gemini
assistant (Cat-Wells). To protect user privacy, an on-device model
converts the camera footage into an anonymized "wireframe" of body-pose
coordinates before anything is sent to Google's servers for translation
(Cat-Wells). SL2T was trained on more than 100,000 hours of data across
over 50 sign languages, with roughly a quarter of that data specific to
ASL, and Google says it built the tool with direct input from Deaf
Googlers and an AI Sign Language Advisory Committee that includes the
National Association of the Deaf and the World Federation of the Deaf
(Cat-Wells). Even so, the article notes real concerns within the Deaf
community about whether the model can handle diverse ASL dialects and
about the tool's current exclusivity to one phone model.

## Annotations

- First sign-language AI feature to ship as a real phone feature (not a
  lab demo or accessibility add-on app) — works inside Gboard and Live
  Transcribe on Pixel 11.
- Privacy design: raw video is not transmitted; only anonymized pose/
  wireframe coordinates are sent to servers.
- Training scale: 100,000+ hours, 50+ sign languages, ~25% ASL-specific.
- Built with direct Deaf community involvement (Deaf Googlers + advisory
  committee of major Deaf organizations) — not designed for the
  community without the community.
- Key tension raised in the article: powerful, community-informed
  technology that is currently locked to a single, expensive device —
  raising equity-of-access questions.

## Personal Response & Questions

I like that Google involved Deaf organizations directly instead of just
building this and shipping it — that seems like the right way to design
accessibility technology. But limiting it to one specific phone model
right away feels like it undercuts the "for Deaf people" framing in the
headline, since not every Deaf person can afford or wants that particular
device.

1. If SL2T currently only ships on the Pixel 11 and only about a quarter
   of its training data is ASL-specific, how long realistically before
   this kind of tool is accurate and available across other phones and
   other sign languages?
2. What happens if the AI misreads a sign during a high-stakes
   conversation — a job interview or a doctor's visit, for example — and
   who is responsible for that error if the user might not even know it
   happened?
